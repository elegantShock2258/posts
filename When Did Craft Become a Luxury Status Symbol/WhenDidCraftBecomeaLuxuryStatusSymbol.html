When Did Craft Become a Luxury Status Symbol?

We went from hiding the hands that made our clothes to making them the most expensive part of the purchase.

I think one of the more interesting things to have happened in Indian fashion over the last few years is the sudden enthusiasm for knowing who made our clothes. For a very long time, Indian artisans were essentially the invisible workforce behind luxury fashion. India was often referred to as the world's embroidery room, producing elaborate handwork for European fashion houses, while the words "Made in India" rarely made it onto the label, let alone the credit line. Now, the very same craftsmanship is being used to justify some rather extraordinary price tags.

Take the Fendi Baguette, for example. One version, featuring 39,500 beads and 138 hours of mirror-work-style craftsmanship, retails for approximately ₹8.3 lakh. Meanwhile, an authentic pair of Kolhapuri chappals, which became the subject of considerable controversy when Prada presented its own version, can be bought on Indian streets for around ₹500. I think that difference is worth sitting with for a moment. The craft has not necessarily changed. What has changed is the context in which we are buying it.

A Kolhapuri artisan at work. The skill and labour behind traditional footwear existed long before luxury brands began assigning it luxury price tags.

There is actually some research behind why handmade products are suddenly so desirable. Studies have found that consumers tend to perceive handmade products as more natural and authentic than identical machine-made ones, and are consequently willing to pay more for them. In luxury fashion, this has become almost a selling principle. Brands increasingly place artisans at the centre of their campaigns, and research suggests that knowing more about the people behind a product can increase its perceived value. Harper's Bazaar India put it rather neatly: rarity now comes from human skill rather than logos.

I think India is experiencing a particularly interesting version of this shift. According to Ipsos's 2026 luxury study, the Indian luxury consumer is becoming what it describes as "post-aspirational". Provenance is beginning to matter more than price, and supporting artisan communities is itself becoming a status symbol. The ultimate flex, apparently, is no longer simply what you can afford to buy, but what you choose to value.

Brands have picked up on this, too. 11.11, for instance, includes the name of the artisan who handspun the khadi in its jeans. Rahul Mishra has also pointed out that the wages involved in producing a sari worn by Zendaya amounted to nearly 30 percent of its price. These are quite important developments, especially in an industry where the people responsible for the most labour-intensive parts of a garment have historically received very little recognition.

But I do think there is a rather uncomfortable question that comes with all this celebration of craft. Who is actually benefiting from it?

The Prada Kolhapuri controversy and the discussion around Fendi's use of Gujarati mirror-work aesthetics without naming Chanakya brought up a familiar issue. Are these luxury houses elevating Indian craft, or are they simply extracting its aesthetic value? There is even a term for the more performative version of this: craftwashing. Researchers use it to describe the way brands can tell stories about handmade production while leaving out important details about who made the product and who actually benefits from its sale

Fendi’s embroidered Baguette, featuring 39,500 beads and 475 mirrors. The bag turns hours of intricate handwork into part of the luxury proposition.

This is particularly important because we seem to have developed an almost automatic association between handmade, sustainable, and ethical. A 2026 survey of Indian couture points out that this equation is inadequate without measurable standards. Just because something has been made by hand does not automatically mean the person who made it has been paid fairly, or that the process is environmentally responsible.  

So, when did craft become a luxury status symbol? I am not entirely convinced that it suddenly did. Craft has always required skill, time and considerable knowledge. What has changed is that the luxury industry has finally recognised that these things can be sold as part of the product itself.

I do think it is a good thing that consumers are becoming more interested in provenance and the people behind their clothes. But I would like to see us become equally interested in what happens to the money we are spending. It is one thing to pay a premium because something has been handmade. It is another to make sure that the person whose hands made it actually receives a meaningful share of that premium.

Otherwise, we have just found another way to make craft expensive for the buyer, valuable for the brand, and underpaid for the person sitting at the loom.
