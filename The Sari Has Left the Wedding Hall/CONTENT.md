# **The Sari Has Left the Wedding Hall**

### ***How the six-yard drape went from being something we saved for special occasions to something we actually want to wear.***

I have always found it quite interesting how our relationship with the sari changes depending on what stage of life we are at. For a lot of urban Indian women, the sari follows a rather predictable trajectory. You wear it as a child because your mother wants you dressed up for a family function, abandon it somewhere around college in favour of jeans and kurtas, and then retrieve it, preferably in silk and with a considerable amount of starch, for your own wedding. It is almost as though the sari is something you graduate into, rather than something you actually choose to wear. At least, that is how it used to be.

For most of the last half century, the sari has been steadily losing its place as everyday clothing. Curators of London's Design Museum exhibition, *The Offbeat Sari*, documented how, from the 1960s to the early 2000s, younger women began associating the garment with the domestic sphere and their previous generations' ideas of propriety \[1\]. The sari was still around, of course. It just had a designated place in our wardrobes, and that place was usually the wedding hall.

I think what is particularly interesting about its revival is that it didn't really happen because someone decided that we needed to preserve an Indian garment. Around 2015, curator Priya Khanchandani noticed Delhi designers experimenting with the sari, while younger women were beginning to wear it as everyday fashion, sometimes with nothing more complicated than a T-shirt and sneakers \[2\]. Her exhibition documented this shift through new drapes, unusual materials and women using the sari to express identities that didn't necessarily fit into conventional ideas of how the garment should be worn. There was even a Benarasi collection photographed on women playing cricket \[1\]. I think that is quite a lovely image, actually.

 *Anavila Misra's contemporary interpretation of the sari, combining a printed linen drape with trousers and a relaxed silhouette. The garment begins to move beyond its conventional styling.*                     

Social media had quite a bit to do with it, too. The \#100SareePact, started in 2015 by two friends in Bengaluru who pledged to wear their saris a hundred times, became an internet sensation. It turned something as ordinary as getting dressed into a way of documenting personal stories, while also bringing traditional weaves back into conversations. Then came dancer Eshna Kutty's viral hula-hooping video, in which she wore a sari with wide-legged pants and trainers. Suddenly, there were young women everywhere trying out the same casual drape.

![][image2]

*Dancer Eshna Kutty hula-hooping in a sari and sneakers. Her viral performance offered a rather convincing demonstration that the six-yard drape need not be reserved for formal occasions.* 

Naturally, an entire design economy has begun developing around this renewed interest. Anavila Misra's linen saris, Sanjay Garg's Raw Mango and Ayush Kejriwal's designs have all contributed to making the sari feel more accessible to the everyday woman. Save the Loom's Vidhi line was originally designed for female lawyers. There is also a growing market for ready-to-drape and pre-stitched saris, which can apparently be worn in under a minute and are growing at 8 to 10 percent annually, faster than the traditional market. Gen Z has taken things a step further, pairing saris with bomber jackets, corset tops and sneakers, and treating the garment almost like a blank canvas to embellish, trim and dye according to their own preferences.

*Contemporary sari designs on display at London's Design Museum exhibition, The Offbeat Sari (2023). The exhibition explored how designers and wearers are pushing the garment beyond conventional forms.* 

But I don't think the most interesting part of this revival is what people are doing to the sari. It is what they are doing to the idea of wearing one.

For years, urban middle-class women have had to negotiate the sari's association with tradition and respectability against their desire to dress in a way that feels modern. You could wear one, but there was always an expectation attached to it. The everyday sari seems to be doing away with that negotiation altogether. It isn't being worn because it is culturally important, or because someone has decided that it is time to reconnect with their roots. It is being worn because it works with the life someone already has. You can cycle in it, work in it, dance in it, and apparently even hula-hoop in it.

I don't think the wedding hall has lost the sari. It has simply lost its monopoly over it. And perhaps that is the most significant part of this entire revival. The sari is finally being allowed to exist outside the occasions we have assigned to it. I would much rather see it become an ordinary part of someone's wardrobe than have it remain something we spend thousands on, wear once, photograph extensively and then put away for the next wedding.
