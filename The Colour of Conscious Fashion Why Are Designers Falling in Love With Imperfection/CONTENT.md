# **The Colour of Conscious Fashion: Why Are Designers Falling in Love With Imperfection?**

### ***When did uneven dyes, irregular prints and faded fabrics become things we actively look for?***

I think we have reached a rather interesting point in fashion where things that would have previously been considered mistakes are now being sold as design features. Unevenly dyed fabrics, faded finishes, irregular prints and all sorts of little inconsistencies are suddenly desirable. And not just desirable, but sometimes considerably more expensive than their perfectly uniform counterparts.

Mridu Mehra of the textile label Kokun describes an imperfect garment as something that carries the fingerprint of life and human hands, rather than the sterile uniformity of a machine. She also explains that her collections begin with what the earth is offering at a particular moment, rather than with trend boards. I quite like this approach, but it does make me wonder whether we are looking at a genuine shift in the way designers approach textiles, or whether imperfection has simply become another aesthetic that can be packaged and sold.

![][image1]

 *Garments by Kokūn, founded by textile artist Mridu Mehra. Its naturally dyed and botanically printed textiles embrace variations in colour and surface as part of their identity.* 

The difference becomes clearer when you look at natural dyes.

Industrial production is largely built around uniformity. Every batch is expected to look the same, and synthetic colours can be controlled to produce consistent results. Natural dyes don't really work that way. The yellow you get from a naturally dyed fabric depends on the marigolds themselves, the temperature of the water and even the hands of the person doing the dyeing. The same process repeated twice might not produce exactly the same result.

![][image2]

*Natural dyeing is shaped by the materials and conditions of the process. The plant, water temperature, dye concentration and handling can all influence the final colour.* 

Brands working with botanical dyes are quite open about this. They explain that they don't control the final outcome or demand symmetry, and that what might traditionally be considered an error, such as an over-boiled eucalyptus leaf, can instead become a feature that speaks of honesty. Indian label Adheera, for example, works with wildflowers and leaf litter to produce colours that are imperfect in a rather lovely way, with no two pieces looking exactly alike.

![][image3]

*Adheera experiments with botanical dyeing, bundle dyeing and Shibori to create one-of-a-kind textiles. Wildflowers, leaves and other natural materials leave their own impressions on the fabric.* 

I think it is important to recognise that this approach isn't particularly new to Indian textiles. Our handloom traditions have never been entirely concerned with achieving the kind of machine-made uniformity that industrial production values so highly.

Take kala cotton, for example. It is an indigenous, rain-fed cotton from Kutch that is naturally coarse and irregular. Rather than relying on chemical treatments to make it conform to industrial standards, traditional methods are used to soften it. The Bhujodi weaving community also works with extra-weft patterns on pit looms, where producing a single shawl can take up to a month . Even labels like Maku deliberately incorporate details that might otherwise be perceived as imperfections, such as reverse buttons and irregular prints.

![][image4]

*Kala cotton, an indigenous rain-fed cotton from Kutch, retains a distinctive natural texture. Its irregularity is not an effect added during finishing, but a characteristic of the fibre itself.* 

In a way, what industrialisation taught us to call flaws is now being recognised as evidence of where something came from and how it was made.

However, I don't think we should be too quick to celebrate every irregular-looking garment as an example of conscious fashion. The aesthetic itself can be manufactured. A factory can print an irregular pattern onto fabric, reproduce the appearance of fading and distressing, and sell something that looks handmade without any of the time or processes usually associated with it.

There is actually a rather interesting contradiction here. Research on secondhand and craft consumption suggests that consumers are often looking for something unique, something that feels as though it has a history \[35\]. They read irregularity as a sign of authenticity. But that is precisely what makes the aesthetic so easy to imitate. If consumers have learnt to associate unevenness with authenticity, it is only a matter of time before mass-produced products start imitating it.

I think this is where we need to distinguish between something that is genuinely imperfect and something that has simply been designed to look that way.

A naturally dyed fabric varies because of the material, the weather, the harvest and the process. An irregular print produced in a factory is still a controlled outcome. Both might look similar when photographed, but the reasons behind them are entirely different.

So, I do think the movement towards imperfection is a real and interesting development. But I would be more interested in the designers whose products cannot help but be imperfect, rather than the ones who have decided that imperfection is the look they want to sell this season.

Because if the irregularity can be reproduced exactly, is it really irregularity anymore? At some point, we have to stop looking at what a fabric looks like and start asking what actually happened to make it that way.
