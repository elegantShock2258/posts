Why Is Fashion Obsessed With Making Our Homes Look Like Us?

From clothes to cushions, it seems we no longer want to just dress ourselves. We want to dress our houses, too.

I think there is something quite funny about walking into a fashion designer's store and finding yourself looking at everything except the clothes. Take Sabyasachi's New Delhi flagship, for example. Before you even get to the clothing, you are greeted by around 300 artworks, antique carpets and even a Bengal tram. The designer himself describes his stores as living museums. His Kolkata mansion makes things even more interesting. He has admitted that he gets much more excited talking about interiors than fashion, and has previously considered extending his work into furniture, antiques, homeware and even hotels. Clearly, he wasn't joking about the interiors.

Sabyasachi's New Delhi flagship is conceived as a living museum, bringing together artworks, antique furniture, carpets and objects collected from different periods. The store offers an experience that extends well beyond fashion.

This fashion-to-home transition is becoming quite a familiar thing in Indian design. And I think it says something about how we have started consuming aesthetics, and more importantly, how much of our personal identity we now want to express through the things we own.

The idea itself is hardly new. International luxury houses have been extending their identities into interiors, hospitality and everyday objects for years. Ralph Lauren has its interiors, Gucci has its cafés, and so on. India has been developing its own version of this for quite some time, too. JJ Valaya launched Valaya Home in 1996, working with embroidered tapestries and furniture. Abraham and Thakore have had a homeware offering almost as long as their fashion label has existed. More recently, we have seen everything from Sabyasachi's tableware collaboration with Thomas Goode to Falguni Shane Peacock's rug collection.

JJ Valaya has even suggested that any serious fashion player will eventually become a lifestyle brand. I think we are beginning to see exactly what he meant.

What makes this transition particularly easy for Indian designers is that the language of textiles already moves quite comfortably between our wardrobes and our homes. Handloom, block printing, embroidery and other textile techniques have never really belonged exclusively to clothing. The same kinds of fabrics and motifs that we use for saris and garments have also been used for furnishings for generations. The sari pallu and the cushion cover are not exactly strangers to each other.

But the way designers are presenting this relationship has changed.

When Asian Paints collaborated with Sabyasachi on Nilaya, the resulting collections, including Thar, Makhmal, Soofa, Hazaribagh and Chowk, translated the same visual vocabulary found in his clothing into home interiors. Coromandel Coast-inspired painted fabrics and old-world jaal patterns moved from saris to sofas and walls. There is a reason this works so well. As one profile observed, Sabyasachi does not simply sell you a sari or a plate. He sells you a small part of the lifestyle that comes with it.

Sabyasachi x Asian Paints for Nilaya translates the designer's signature visual vocabulary into home furnishings. Rich floral upholstery, traditional patterns and antique-inspired objects bring the aesthetic of his fashion collections into domestic spaces.

Nicobar took a similar approach from the beginning. Its founders wanted to create an entire world and way of living, rather than just a collection of products. Newer labels such as Matsya Home & Living are making this ambition even more explicit, extending their idea of modern royalty across dinnerware, crystal and soft furnishings, and describing their evolution as a move from couture house to lifestyle curator.

Nicobar approaches design as an interconnected lifestyle, bringing together homeware, ceramics, textiles and everyday objects within a consistent visual identity.

I think the pandemic had something to do with making this shift feel even more natural. When we suddenly began spending so much more time inside our homes, the way those spaces looked became considerably more important. Even companies that were previously associated with paint began positioning themselves as integrated home decor players.

And perhaps this is just the next stage of something we have already been doing for years. We started with the outfit of the day, carefully putting together how we wanted to look and how we wanted other people to perceive us. Now, apparently, the same thought process extends to our living rooms. The house has become another place where we perform our personal aesthetic.

There is also a rather interesting commercial benefit to this. Fashion is an industry that moves incredibly quickly. Trends come and go, collections are replaced, and something that was desirable six months ago can suddenly feel dated. Interiors, on the other hand, are generally expected to last much longer. By moving into homeware, designers are taking the identity-building power of fashion and placing it in a space where people are more likely to hold on to things.

I suppose we have reached a point where good taste is no longer something we want to display only when we get dressed. We want it in our dining rooms, on our walls, in the cups we drink from and even in the rugs we walk over. Fashion has moved into our homes, and I don't think it is planning on leaving anytime soon.
