# **The New Heirloom: Why Are We Suddenly Dressing Like Our Grandmothers?**

### ***From grandmother's steel almirah to the modern bridal wardrobe, why old textiles are becoming some of our most coveted possessions.***

![][image1]

I have noticed that a lot of the things we seem to be most excited about wearing these days are things that have already been worn by someone else. And not just anything secondhand, either. We are talking about a grandmother's Kanjeevaram, a Parsi gara sari in an almost impossible shade of turquoise, or a necklace made from Victorian-era gold guineas that a great-grandfather collected back in 1805\. Vogue India has been documenting quite a bit of this lately, featuring young, fashionable urbanites wearing family heirlooms that carry stories of weddings, family histories and other significant moments.

I think there is something quite interesting about the fact that we are suddenly treating our grandparents' wardrobes as fashion inspiration. Of course, the obvious explanation is sustainability. We have all become increasingly aware of the amount of clothing being produced, consumed and eventually discarded, and the vintage fashion movement has largely been associated with a reaction against mass-produced fast fashion and a growing interest in environmental sustainability. Tarun Tahiliani, for instance, has encouraged his clients to wear and rewear their purchases, pointing to the 92 million tonnes of clothing that end up in landfills. There are also businesses like I Was A Sari, which have built their model around rescuing second-hand saris from Mumbai's Chor Bazaar.

But I don't think sustainability completely explains why we are so drawn to old clothes.

Research on vintage consumers suggests that the main motivations are often nostalgia, an interest in fashion and the desire to own something unique. There is even an element of treasure hunting involved. While someone shopping for secondhand clothing might primarily be looking for a good price, a vintage shopper is often looking for something with a story that cannot be replicated. Ethnographic research on vintage shops makes a similar observation: garments become valuable because they are treated as objects from another time, carrying stories about how people used to live, repair and pass things down. Quite often, these stories are attached to the figure of a mother or grandmother. A vintage garment, as one researcher puts it, is never just valuable.

But I think India has a slightly different relationship with this entire idea. We haven't exactly discovered heirlooms. We have been keeping them in cupboards for generations.

The practice of folding away saris, bringing them out for special occasions and eventually passing them down has long been one of the most carefully preserved traditions of the Indian family wardrobe. I think most Indian women would recognise the image of a grandmother's steel almirah, complete with wooden rods for hanging saris, and the rather overwhelming collection of clothes that somehow never seemed to get thrown away. These garments were not necessarily kept because someone had decided that vintage was fashionable. They were kept because they belonged to someone, because they were expensive to make, because they were associated with a particular occasion, or simply because nobody thought there was any reason to get rid of them.

What I find genuinely new is what younger generations are beginning to do with these inherited textiles.

Instead of wearing their grandmother's sari exactly as it was, brides are increasingly transforming these fabrics into gowns, ghaghras, jackets and other forms of occasionwear. One bride described the process as a way of honouring the women in her family while making new memories of her own with the same fabric. There is also the example of a 150-year-old Mysore silk sari becoming a connection between India and Britain. Even heirloom gold is being melted down and reset into new wedding bands.

I think this is quite different from simply buying vintage clothing because it is fashionable or because it is better for the environment. There is an existing relationship with the material, and the person wearing it is not just acquiring something old. They are deciding what happens to it next.

Perhaps that is why I find this particular shift so interesting from a textile perspective. We spend so much time discussing how traditional textiles can be made relevant to younger consumers, how old techniques can be translated into contemporary products and how craft can find a place in modern wardrobes. But sometimes the material already exists. It has been sitting in someone's cupboard for thirty years, waiting for someone to decide what to do with it.

The West may have discovered vintage as an alternative to disposable fashion, but I think India's relationship with heirlooms is doing something slightly different. We are taking something that already has a history and giving ourselves permission to change it. A grandmother's sari does not have to remain a sari forever. It can become a jacket, a wedding outfit or something entirely different, without necessarily losing what made it important in the first place.

And perhaps that is the real luxury here. Not simply owning something old, or being able to afford something that was made by hand, but having something that belongs to your family and being able to make it your own. It is a rather lovely thought that a piece of fabric can belong to several generations without having to look exactly the same for any of them.
