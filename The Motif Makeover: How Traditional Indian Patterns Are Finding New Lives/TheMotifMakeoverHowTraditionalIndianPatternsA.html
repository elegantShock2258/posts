The Motif Makeover: How Traditional Indian Patterns Are Finding New Lives

Our traditional motifs are being borrowed, reworked and reimagined. But what exactly makes something recognisably Indian?

I have always found it interesting how certain motifs manage to survive across generations, even when the objects they originally belonged to have changed completely. A Guttapusalu necklace, for instance, is a traditional South Indian pearl-fringe heirloom. But designer Aisha Rao has taken its familiar pearl clusters and translated them into what she describes as her signature botanical language, using gathered pearls and stones that resemble the delicate details of textile trims. She has also reinterpreted the traditional mango motif through a more contemporary lens.

The traditional South Indian Guttapusalu necklace is recognised for its distinctive clusters of pearls, which form a delicate fringe around the jewellery. Its ornamental language offers a starting point for contemporary reinterpretations.

Elsewhere, Falguni Shane Peacock has been looking towards the marble peacocks and jali patterns found in Baroda's Laxmi Vilas Palace and Jaipur's City Palace, translating these architectural details into couture garments that have been described as moving artefacts .

Falguni Shane Peacock's Raj Mahal Bijoux collection, presented at India Couture Week 2025, draws on India's royal visual heritage. Intricate peacock imagery and elaborate ornamentation translate the grandeur of historic architecture into contemporary couture.

I think we are seeing traditional Indian motifs everywhere at the moment. But I am more interested in how they are being used than in simply celebrating their return.

A lot of the vocabulary being revisited is considerably older than we might realise. Jaal work, for example, consists of dense networks of floral patterns and has long been used in garments such as the 400-year-old Khada Dupatta. Its body is traditionally decorated with jaal patterns, kundan work and floral vines. Research into heritage motif revival also traces these patterns back to mythology, religion, folklore and everyday life. Even something as familiar as the paisley motif carries associations with fertility, infinity and continuity, meanings that continue to exist even when the motif is used in an entirely different context.

What I find particularly interesting is that designers are no longer necessarily reproducing these motifs exactly as they appeared in their original forms. Instead, there seems to be a growing interest in translating them.

One way of doing this is by retaining the original motif but changing its proportions, scale or application. Aisha Rao's jewellery is an example of this approach, where heritage motifs are reinterpreted rather than reproduced literally. The idea is to create something that feels contemporary enough to be worn regularly, while still retaining a connection to its original inspiration.

Jewellery is perhaps where this works particularly well. A lot of the newer modern-heirloom pieces take traditional Indian elements and make them smaller, lighter or easier to wear. The intention is to create something that can be worn in either the East or the West, and that does not necessarily belong to one particular season or occasion.

The second approach is slightly more interesting to me. Instead of simply changing the appearance of a motif, some designers are questioning whether it needs to retain its original form at all.

Designer Bhavya Ramesh has spoken about wanting to move away from the traditional forms associated with Indian jewellery. Her intention is to create pieces that feel Indian in spirit, but not necessarily in appearance. I think this is quite an interesting distinction. A motif does not always have to look exactly like a paisley, or a mango, or a flower, to retain something of the visual language it came from.

Bhavya Ramesh challenges conventional ideas of Indian jewellery through sculptural forms that extend beyond traditional ornamentation. Her work explores how cultural identity can be expressed through shape, material and craftsmanship without relying on familiar motifs.

Both approaches seem to be responding to the same problem. Traditional Indian jewellery is often considered too ceremonial for everyday wear, while contemporary jewellery can sometimes feel as though it is missing a distinctly Indian identity. The same sort of negotiation is happening in clothing, too. Emerging Indian labels are reworking bandhani, chikankari and traditional weaving techniques into sharper, more contemporary silhouettes, with the intention of making them feel relevant rather than nostalgic.

But I do think there is a point at which reinterpretation can become a little too convenient.

We have seen this happen with Indian craft often enough. A motif gets separated from the community, history and technique that gave it meaning in the first place, and eventually all that remains is a recognisable aesthetic. It becomes something that can be printed onto anything and sold without any real understanding of where it came from.

I don't think traditional motifs need to be preserved exactly as they are. In fact, I would argue that the reason many of them have survived for so long is because they have been allowed to change. But there is a difference between taking inspiration from something and simply taking its appearance.

A jali pattern translated from a palace into a garment, or a pearl cluster reimagined as botanical jewellery, can be a rather lovely way of keeping these visual languages alive. The motif doesn't have to remain attached to the same object forever. It just needs to retain enough of its original meaning to have something worth saying.

Perhaps that is the real point of a motif makeover. Not to make something old look new, but to give it a reason to exist in a completely different context.
