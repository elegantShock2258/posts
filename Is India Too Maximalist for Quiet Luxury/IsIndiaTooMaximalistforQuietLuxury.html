Is India Too Maximalist for Quiet Luxury?

Between our love for ornament and our newfound obsession with restraint, what exactly are we trying to prove?

I have noticed that somewhere along the way, we seem to have developed a rather complicated relationship with what Indian luxury is supposed to look like. On one end, we have the beige Sabyasachi bridal sari, with its understated colour palette and layers of intricate handwork. On the other, we have the elaborate zardozi, the heavy jewellery, the colours and the general willingness to put as much as possible on a single surface. And somewhere in between, we have quiet luxury, which has spent the last few years convincing the rest of the world that looking expensive is best achieved by looking as though you haven't tried very hard.

I find this particularly interesting in the Indian context because our textile and craft traditions have never exactly been shy about ornamentation. An account of the Mughal karkhana workshops describes an approach to decoration that aimed to cover the maximum possible surface with the maximum possible detail. So when we suddenly find ourselves discussing whether Indian luxury should be more minimal, I think it is worth asking where this expectation is coming from in the first place.

A close-up of zardozi embroidery, with densely worked metallic threads and floral motifs. Ornamentation has long been an important part of India's textile vocabulary.

Sabyasachi Mukherjee has been quite vocal about this. He has previously questioned why a country as exuberant as India would want to pursue minimalism at all. In a more recent essay, he makes an interesting distinction between two parallel aesthetic traditions that have existed here for centuries: the spiritual minimalism of introspective traditions and the material maximalism that came from mercantile abundance. He argues that our discomfort with ornamentation is, at least partly, inherited from colonialism, which encouraged Indian designers to borrow foreign ideas of sophistication.

What I find interesting is that even Sabyasachi, whose work is hardly an exercise in minimalism, doesn't seem to believe that maximalism is simply about adding more things. He describes it as something sensory and tactile, rather than merely visual, and argues that restraint is actually at the centre of it. Which, if you think about it, makes quite a bit of sense. There is a difference between something being elaborate and something being poorly considered.

But I don't think the people working with restraint in India are necessarily borrowing from the West, either. Ruchika Sachdeva of Bodice points out that while colour and embellishment are deeply embedded in Indian aesthetics, there has always been another language of minimalism here. Khadi, handwoven silks and minimal weaving techniques are all examples of it. Sanjay Garg of Raw Mango, who has shown at London Fashion Week, makes an equally interesting point: no country owns minimalism, and he wants to show the world what his interpretation of Indian aesthetics and refinement looks like. Delhi-based label Lovebirds has also described its approach to daywear as being built around restraint, something it considers representative of a new India.

A restrained interpretation of Indian handwoven textiles from Raw Mango's AGAMA collection. Subtle colour, texture and the character of the weave take precedence over surface embellishment. 

I suppose the problem is that we keep treating these two approaches as though they are mutually exclusive. They really aren't.

Rahul Mishra calls this emerging sensibility mindful luxury, which is essentially about consuming with more thought. Aine Arora of péro describes it as a maximalist approach to creating quiet luxury: the finished product might not be particularly loud, but the process, the craftsmanship and the effort behind it are all very much present. Even Sabyasachi's beige bridal sari for Aditi Rao Hydari, which was widely celebrated for its minimalist appearance, involved layers of intricate handwork. It was hardly minimal in the amount of labour that went into making it.

I think this is where the conversation becomes more interesting for us as people working with textiles. A fabric doesn't necessarily have to be colourful, heavily embellished or visually busy to demonstrate the amount of skill that went into making it. A fine handwoven silk, a piece of khadi or a quiet zari detail can require just as much consideration. And perhaps the fact that these things are now being recognised as luxury by an international audience is less about India adopting Western minimalism and more about us recognising a part of our own design vocabulary that we haven't always given enough attention to.

India is now the world's fifth-largest economy, and there is an increasingly vocal diaspora demanding that Indian crafts receive the credit and recognition they deserve. I think that has something to do with the confidence we are beginning to see in the way Indian designers present their work internationally.

However, I do worry about one thing. We seem to be moving towards a point where restraint is considered the more sophisticated option, while density and ornamentation are treated as something we need to explain or justify. If that happens, we haven't really moved away from colonial ideas of sophistication. We have just found a new way of dressing them up.

I don't think India needs to choose between maximalism and minimalism. We have always had room for both. And frankly, I would find it rather boring if our idea of luxury eventually became a wardrobe full of beige clothes and very expensive white shirts.
